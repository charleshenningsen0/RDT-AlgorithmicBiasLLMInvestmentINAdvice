# RDT - Algorithmic Bias in LLM Investment Advice

This repository contains artifacts from my research experience in the BerNE Research Program, an NSF-funded research initiative focused on Responsible Digital Transformation (RDT). The program guided undergraduate researchers through an exploration of digital ethics and the rapidly changing impact of AI and digital technologies on businesses and society.

My research focused on algorithmic bias and personalization in LLM-based financial advice. I explored whether demographic information provided to an LLM could influence the investment recommendations it generates, even when users have the same financial situation.

## What is Responsible Digital Transformation?

Responsible Digital Transformation (RDT) is the process of adopting and integrating digital technologies while considering their ethical, social, and organizational impacts.

Digital transformation is not only about whether a technology can improve efficiency or create new capabilities. Organizations must also consider the potential harms introduced by these technologies and how those harms can be identified, monitored, and mitigated.

My research explored RDT through the growing use of AI in financial services. LLM-based financial tools can make financial information more accessible and personalized, but they can also introduce concerns involving algorithmic bias, privacy, transparency, and over-reliance on AI.

## What Did I Accomplish?

I conducted an exploratory research project investigating how demographic personalization could affect investment advice generated by an LLM.

I began by conducting a literature review on algorithmic bias, LLM-based robo-advisors, personalization, and Responsible Digital Transformation. Using methodologies I found in existing research, I designed an experiment comparing personalized user profiles against a non-personalized control.

I then developed a Python-based experiment using Google's Gemini API. The experiment held financial characteristics such as age, income, savings, and risk tolerance constant while changing demographic characteristics such as name, occupation, location, and marital status. For each profile, the program queried the model multiple times, parsed its structured responses, and collected asset-allocation recommendations for comparison.

The experiment identified differences between some of the personalized profiles and the control. Because of the limited sample size and scope, these findings were exploratory rather than evidence of a causal relationship. This limitation also helped identify potential directions for future work, including larger experiments, isolating individual demographic variables, and studying how real-world LLM personalization and memory affect users.

More than anything, I am proud that I used this opportunity to pursue something I was genuinely interested in: AI research. BerNE gave me my first experience conducting academic research and created a foundation that I hope to build on through future research and technical opportunities.
